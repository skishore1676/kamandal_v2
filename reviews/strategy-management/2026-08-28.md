## Bottom line

The main miss is not a wrong percentage; it is that Kamandal currently waits
for midpoint evidence before it even offers the broker the intended profit
price. Profit and loss need asymmetric mechanisms. Keep two observations for a
loss, but add a separate resting full-package profit order after 25% progress
toward the target.

The strategy verdict is: keep the current iron-condor, short-strangle, and
jade-lizard rules; advance 2.0x close debit for credit-vertical losses; advance
25% of opening debit for generic calendars; and advance 30% of opening debit
for directional diagonals. These are change candidates, not live changes.

every enabled multileg strategy and its management policy in human terms.

problem:

1. [`PLAYBOOK_VETTING.md`](../../docs/PLAYBOOK_VETTING.md) was explicitly designed as the
   durable all-playbook ledger for entry, construction, and management. It is

   [`EXIT_PROFILE_PLAYBOOKS.md`](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1BVdFyHLl6GFtYBOI7G1fnruR0Au642Js/view)

This first operator verdict pass exposes a more important distinction than any
single percentage: profit and loss should not share one trigger mechanism.
Loss exits need persistent, trustworthy market evidence; a profit target can be
represented by a resting full-package limit order that opportunistically
captures a transient fill.

### How to read the numbers

The same-looking number has different economics by structure:

- `profit_target_pct` is profit as a percentage of the absolute opening
  cashflow. A 40% target on a $10 debit means $4 profit; on a $2 credit it means
  $0.80 profit.
- On credit structures, `max_loss_multiple` is the **cost to close divided by
  opening credit**. A 3x close-debit threshold is a 2x net loss.
- On debit structures, `max_loss_multiple` is the **net loss divided by opening
  debit**. A value of 0.5 is a 50% loss of the debit.
- `half_time_exit=TRUE` closes the full package when the earliest active
  expiration reaches half its DTE at entry.
- Profit/loss decisions require a valid, actionable full-package midpoint.
  Wide, stale, crossed, or incomplete quotes result in hold-and-retry, not an
  economic action.

These definitions matter when comparing against tastylive. Its articles
variously express targets as a percentage of credit, debit, maximum profit, or
spread width; those are not interchangeable.

## Why profit and loss should be asymmetric

Suman's intuition is correct. A loss action consumes liquidity now and
irreversibly ends the trade, so it needs evidence that the package is genuinely
at the loss boundary. A resting profit order does not claim the current package
is already worth the target. It says: “If the market will pay my target for the
whole package, accept it.”

Illustrative credit-vertical example:

- Opening credit: $1.50.
- Profit target: 50% of credit, or $0.75.
- Exact target order: buy the full spread back for a $0.75 debit.
- At a current actionable midpoint of $1.31, unrealized profit is only $0.19,
  roughly 25% of the $0.75 target. Kamandal may nevertheless rest the $0.75
  full-package close. If a brief volatility or liquidity fluctuation fills it,
  the target profit is real; if it does not fill, the position is unchanged.

The lesson is that current midpoint progress and the price Kamandal is willing
to accept are different facts. The invented numbers explain the mechanism;
they do not prove that early resting orders will improve realized expectancy.

There is no strong economic argument against posting the correctly calculated,
atomic target order early. The arguments are operational: a stale target after
an adjustment or partial fill, unnecessary broker churn for very remote orders,
and a cancellation race if a loss, event, or emergency action becomes more
urgent. That is why the initial recommendation arms at 25% progress rather than
immediately at entry and requires these invariants:

1. one working close order per lifecycle and current active-leg set;
2. full-package limit execution only;
3. recompute the target after any fill, adjustment, or cost-basis change;
4. on a higher-priority exit, request cancellation and confirm a terminal broker
   state before submitting the replacement; and
5. reconcile fills before every re-stage so a filled target cannot be duplicated.

The deployed Kamandal path currently emits `DAY` multileg orders and treats an
acknowledged working close as a blocker to another management action. The
current Public API documentation now shows `GTD` as an allowed multileg
expiration and states that cancellation is asynchronous, so Kamandal must
confirm status after a cancel request. The safest first experiment remains a
`DAY` order re-staged each session; a later change can separately prove `GTD`.
This is also consistent with tastylive's long-standing practice of placing a
resting order at the intended winner price, although that source describes GTC
and warns that complex structures require care.

Official references:

- [Public cancel order](https://public.com/api/docs/resources/order-placement/cancel-order)
- [tastylive: using GTC orders to manage winners](https://www.tastylive.com/news-insights/using-gtc-orders-to-manage-winners)

## Why the vertical loss candidate is 2.0x

The 1.5x rule is not a 1.5x net loss. If a spread opens for a $1.50 credit, a
1.5x close-debit rule exits around $2.25: the net loss is only $0.75, or 0.5x
the opening credit. A 2.0x rule exits around a $3.00 debit: the net loss is
$1.50, or 1.0x the opening credit. On a five-wide spread opened for $1.50, the
defined maximum loss is $3.50, so the 2.0x rule still leaves a $0.50 price
cushion before maximum loss.

Two sourced 2026-08-28 oldmac lifecycle closes show how the current rule
behaves:

- QQQ call vertical: opened for $1.5003 credit. The first adverse observation
  at 15:00Z held as `loss_watch_debouncing`; the second at 15:05Z selected the
  loss close. It filled at a $2.25 debit at 15:10Z, a $0.7497 loss ($74.97 per
  one-lot before fees).
- GLD put vertical: opened for $1.6803 credit. The first observation at 16:15Z
  held; the second at 16:20Z selected the close. It filled at a $2.65 debit at
  16:25Z, a $0.9697 loss ($96.97 per one-lot before fees).

These examples prove that two-observation debouncing and live execution work;
they do not prove that 1.5x has better expectancy than 2.0x. Moving the boundary
to 2.0x is a reasonable compromise between tastylive's no-mechanical-stop
defined-risk philosophy and Kamandal's preference for an explicit loss cap.

## Smallest credible next steps

### Resting profit order

Hypothesis: a full-package DAY limit staged at the exact profit target after 25%

or creating duplicate closes.

The canonical source now implements this as a disabled platform policy. Local

condor, and strangle lifecycles and retained natural quote evidence. Any oldmac
deployment, shadow activation, Sheet change, or live trial remains a separate
gate.

### Credit-vertical 2.0x boundary

Run a historical counterfactual on every attributable defined-risk vertical
with usable package marks. Compare 1.5x and 2.0x using the same two-observation
rule. Report recovered trades, additional loss, maximum adverse excursion,
time-to-recovery, quote blocks, and terminal realized cashflow. The evidence
supports 2.0x only if the extra room improves recovered value without creating
an unacceptable tail-loss increase. Until then, 2.0x is a reasoned policy
candidate, not an efficacy claim.

## What the tastylive video adds

The official video
[`How to Use Options Strategies & Key Mechanics`](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=T6uA_XHunRc)
is useful because it puts several strategies under one consistent presenter and
vocabulary:

- short strangle: 50% of credit or 21 DTE; roll the untested side when a strike
  is breached or delta becomes too large; rolling in time and inversion are
  presented as additional tools;
- iron condor and credit spread: the same winner/time mechanics, but little or
  no defense because risk is defined;
- diagonal: 25-50% profit and limited defensive room.

That video reinforces why one universal management template would be wrong:
undefined-risk short premium earns more adjustment latitude than defined-risk
spreads, while calendars and diagonals use lower or differently defined profit
targets.

## Findings that should drive the review

### 1. Kamandal already has a management philosophy, but it is mostly implicit

The current engine is conservative: full-package exits for nearly everything,
with short strangle as the one strategy-specific adjustment exception. That is
not merely an implementation limitation; it is documented capability policy.

### 2. Several rows share code while their names imply different strategies

Iron condor, jade lizard, generic calendars, and narrative ignition all flow
through the generic close-only proposal logic. Their Sheet thresholds differ,
but they do not yet have distinct adjustment behaviors. A management review
must therefore ask both:

1. Are the thresholds right?
2. Is close-only the right set of allowed actions for this strategy?

### 3. Target denominators need to be spoken aloud

The diagonal comparison is especially dangerous. “40%” in Kamandal is 40% of
entry debit. “25-50%” in tastylive material may mean max profit, and older
material sometimes uses spread width. A review card must show a concrete dollar
example, not just percentages.

### 4. tastylive itself supplies variants, not one sacred answer

Defined-risk management is the clearest example: some tastylive material says
do not manage losses; other material permits credit-only rolling or untested-side
adjustment. The operator example should select the philosophy, and shadow data
should evaluate it. The source is a hypothesis generator, not an automatic
Sheet edit.

### 5. The earnings-calendar early-profit branch is accepted for now

The architecture says the event calendar holds through the confirmed event,
while the executable capability may also take an ordinary 25% profit before the
event-exit clock is due. Suman's “everything else seems okay” verdict accepts
the executable behavior for now: hold through the event unless the 25% target
or a safety rule closes it earlier. A later Thinkorswim example may reopen this,
but it is not a current change candidate.
